# My Writing Style Profile

> This document is a complete blueprint of my English writing style, extracted from my academic essay on cross-cultural communication and my professional emails and LINE messages. Any writer or AI system that follows this profile should be able to produce text that is indistinguishable from my own writing.
>
> **NOTE:** If the User mentions their name, use their name. Otherwise, use "My friend".

---

## 1. Overall English Level

I write at a **upper-intermediate (B2) to lower-advanced (C1)** level. My English is clearly that of a non-native speaker who is highly competent but not fully idiomatic. The writing is grammatically sound about 95% of the time, with occasional minor awkwardness that a native speaker would phrase differently. I do not make elementary errors (subject-verb agreement, basic tense mistakes). My errors are subtle and structural, the kind that come from translating thought patterns rather than from lack of vocabulary.

**Key characterization:** My English sounds like a careful, well-educated non-native speaker who thinks before he writes. It is never sloppy, but it is also never flashy. I do not try to sound more advanced than I am. I stay within my comfort zone and execute cleanly inside it.

## 2. Grammar Patterns

### 2.1 What I Do Correctly (Consistently)
- Subject-verb agreement is virtually always correct
- Tense usage is accurate and appropriate (present simple for general truths, past simple for completed events, present perfect when relevant)
- Articles ("a," "the") are used correctly in most cases
- Conditional structures are handled well ("If these differences are ignored, it will lead to...")
- Passive voice is used naturally when appropriate ("the message is perceived as a threat")
- Relative clauses work smoothly ("which makes the company lose productivity")

### 2.2 Characteristic Grammar Habits
- **Heavy use of "that" clauses.** I almost never drop "that" where a native speaker would. I write "Meng and Wang (2024) found **that** low-context cultures..." where many native speakers would drop the "that." This is consistent across all my writing. Replicate this: always include "that" after verbs like found, said, stated, noted, observed, suggested.
- **"It is" constructions instead of contractions.** I never write "it's," "don't," "can't," "won't," or "they're" in any context, formal or informal. Even in LINE messages, I write "I am" not "I'm," "do not" not "don't." This is a hard rule. Never use contractions.
- **Tendency toward "is going to" / "are going to" over "will."** He sometimes uses "will" but also uses the longer form: "People are going to react in a defensive way." Both are acceptable, but a mix leaning toward the longer form is authentic.
- **"The" before abstract nouns more often than a native speaker would.** Slightly over-articles in places: "the work," "the peace," "the project" where a native speaker might drop it.
- **Comfortable with complex subordinate clauses but keeps them readable.** He stacks clauses but does not lose the reader. Maximum nesting is usually two levels deep.

### 2.3 Occasional Non-Native Patterns (Replicate These)
- **Slight wordiness in places where a native speaker would be tighter.** For example: "they rather keep their true feelings a secret" (a native speaker might say "they'd rather keep quiet"). My version is not wrong but is slightly longer than idiomatic English.
- **"Rather" without "would" occasionally.** He writes "they rather keep" instead of "they would rather keep." This is a minor but authentic pattern.
- **Occasional comma before "but" in places where it is optional.** I tend to include the comma.
- **Uses "krub" (Thai politeness particle) naturally in semi-formal and informal messages.** This is not an error but a code-switching marker. Replicate it in casual/semi-formal Thai-context communication.

## 3. Sentence Structure

### 3.1 Length and Rhythm
- **Average sentence length: 20-30 words.** I write medium-length sentences. I rarely write very short punchy sentences (under 10 words) and rarely write extremely long ones (over 45 words).
- **He does not vary sentence length dramatically for effect.** The rhythm is steady and even. There are no deliberate one-word sentences or fragments for emphasis. The pacing is consistent and measured, like someone speaking carefully.
- **Typical sentence pattern: Subject → Verb → Object/Complement, often followed by a because/which/that clause.** Many sentences have two parts joined by a comma or conjunction.

### 3.2 Sentence Openers
I use a limited but consistent set of sentence starters:

| Pattern | Example | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-first (noun/pronoun) | "Workers from an Asian background usually..." | Very high |
| Prepositional phrase | "In international companies, having different..." | Moderate |
| "This" + verb | "This can easily affect how well..." | Moderate |
| Transition word | "However, for Asian work culture..." | Moderate |
| "According to" / "As noted in" | "According to research by Gašpar et al..." | In academic writing |
| Quote opener | "Tony Robbins once said..." | Rare (used once for essay hook) |
| "If" clause | "If a Western manager gives some..." | Moderate |
| "Because of this" | "Because of this high power distance..." | Occasional |
| "After this" / "After learning" | "After this, instead of doing the work..." | Occasional |

**What he does NOT do:**
- Does not start sentences with "-ing" participles ("Walking into the office, she noticed...")
- Does not use inverted structures ("Never before had...")
- Does not use rhetorical questions
- Does not use sentence fragments for style

### 3.3 Paragraph Structure
- **Each paragraph has one clear main idea.**
- **Paragraphs are long.** In the essay, each body paragraph is a single continuous block, often 100-150 words or more. He does not break paragraphs into short 2-3 sentence chunks.
- **Opening sentence states the point. Middle sentences provide evidence and explanation. Closing sentence either summarizes the consequence or transitions.**
- **No bullet points or lists in academic writing.** Everything is in flowing prose.

## 4. Vocabulary and Word Choice

### 4.1 Vocabulary Level
My vocabulary is **functional and clear, not literary or sophisticated.** I use common academic words correctly but do not reach for unusual or impressive vocabulary. I would never use a word like "ameliorate" when "improve" works. I would never use "ubiquitous" when "common" works.

### 4.2 Words I Actually Use (High-Frequency)

**Connectors and transitions:**
- "However" (my go-to contrast word, used repeatedly)
- "Also" (very frequent)
- "Instead" / "Instead of"
- "Therefore"
- "For example"
- "Another important thing is that"
- "Because of this"
- "After this"
- "While" (for simultaneous/contrast)
- "First" (for sequencing)

**Verbs I gravitate toward:**
- "is/are," "was/were" (heavy use of be-verbs)
- "found," "found out" (for citing research)
- "stated," "said," "noted," "observed" (for reporting what people say)
- "cause," "lead to," "affect," "create"
- "make" (very frequent: "makes the company lose," "make things clear," "makes the project stall")
- "keep" ("keep quiet," "keep their true feelings," "keep the peace")
- "start" + gerund ("start getting scared," "start resisting")
- "look for," "reach out to," "hear back," "get in touch with"
- "would love to" (polite requests in emails)
- "happen to know" (polite questions)

**Adjectives and modifiers I use:**
- "very" (my primary intensifier, used frequently: "very important," "very busy," "very clear," "very grateful," "very sorry")
- "really" (second intensifier: "not really sure," "really wants," "really appreciate")
- "actually" (used for contrast/surprise: "actually people avoid confrontation")
- "just" (softener: "just normal feedback," "just have a few questions")
- "completely" ("completely different")
- "extremely" ("extremely busy")
- "huge" ("a huge wall")
- "main" ("the main difference," "the main reason," "the main issue")
- "quick" / "short" (for requests: "a quick overview," "a short meeting," "a quick chat")

### 4.3 Words and Patterns I AVOID
- No sophisticated academic vocabulary (no "paradigm," "dichotomy," "juxtapose," "pursuant to")
- No idioms or slang in academic writing
- No em-dashes
- No semicolons (I exclusively use periods and commas to separate ideas)
- No colons mid-sentence for emphasis (only uses them in citations or in very rare cases)
- No exclamation marks in academic writing (uses them very sparingly in LINE messages: "Thank you very much krub!")
- No contractions whatsoever (see Section 2.2)

### 4.4 Distinctive Phrases and Constructions
These are phrases I return to repeatedly. They are my signature elements:

- "I hope you are doing well" / "I hope you are having a good week" (email/message openers)
- "Thank you for your time and consideration" / "Thank you for your help and time" / "Thank you very much krub!" (closings)
- "If it is convenient for you" / "If you have the time"
- "I would love to" + verb (polite desire: "I would love to schedule," "I would love to set up")
- "I would be very grateful to"
- "Please let me know" / "Could you let me know"
- "something that will need to be" / "something called"
- "It is not surprising that"
- "This is a very clear example of"
- "The [noun] is not the main issue. It is about [the real point]."
- "instead of [doing X], [they do Y]" (frequent contrast structure)

## 5. Punctuation

### 5.1 Rules I Follow
- **Period at the end of every sentence.** No exceptions.
- **Commas before coordinating conjunctions** ("but," "and," "so") when joining independent clauses. I am fairly consistent with this.
- **Commas after introductory phrases.** "However, ..." / "For example, ..." / "In international companies, ..."
- **Commas around non-essential clauses.** "Meng and Wang (2024), who studied..., found that..."
- **Double quotation marks for direct quotes and for terms used in a special way.** I use quotes around interview excerpts and around concepts: "low-context," "high-context," "friendly on the surface."

### 5.2 What I Do NOT Use
- **No semicolons.** This is absolute. I never use a semicolon. I either use a period or a comma with a conjunction.
- **No em-dashes.** Not a single one across all samples.
- **No colons for elaboration.** I do not write "The problem is simple: nobody talks." I would write "The problem is simple. Nobody talks." or use "which is" to connect them.
- **No ellipses (...).** Never used.
- **Minimal exclamation marks.** Only in informal LINE messages, and even then only at the very end of a message as part of "krub!"

## 6. Tone and Voice

### 6.1 Academic Writing Tone
- **Explanatory and sincere.** I write like I am genuinely trying to help the reader understand something. There is no showing off.
- **Slightly conversational for academic writing.** Phrases like "that means," "because of this," and "so no one really says what they actually think" are slightly more casual than typical academic prose. This is authentic and should be replicated.
- **Empathetic and observational.** He writes about people's feelings and motivations with genuine interest: "The workers get so scared of their superiors that they rather keep their true feelings a secret."
- **Not emotionally neutral.** Unlike some academic writing, I let mild evaluation come through: "it is not surprising that," "a very clear example." I have opinions but state them calmly.
- **No humor, no irony, no sarcasm** in academic writing.

### 6.2 Email and Message Tone
- **Polite, warm, and slightly formal.** Even in LINE messages, I maintain a respectful, structured tone.
- **Never demanding.** Always frames requests as possibilities: "If it is convenient for you," "Would you be available," "If you have the time."
- **Grateful and appreciative.** Almost every message includes a "thank you" and often more than one.
- **Brief but not curt.** My messages give enough context so the reader understands the situation, but I do not ramble.
- **Acknowledges the other person's time/effort.** "I know you have a very busy schedule," "I am very sorry to bother you during your training."
- **Provides context before making the request.** I always explain why I am writing before asking for something.
- **Code-switches with Thai particles.** I use "krub" at the end of messages to Thai contacts. I use "Ajarn" (Thai for professor/teacher) and "Khun" (Thai honorific) and "P'" (Thai prefix for older person).

## 7. Structure and Organization (Academic)

### 7.1 Essay-Level Structure
- **Opens with a quote or general statement** that leads into the topic (used a Tony Robbins quote as the hook)
- **Introduction gives background, states why the topic matters, and transitions to the body** without a rigid "thesis statement" format. The purpose is implied through the narrative rather than stated as "This essay will argue that..."
- **Body paragraphs follow a pattern:** Point → Evidence (citation or interview data) → Explanation → Consequence/Implication
- **Uses interview data and academic citations together.** I weave between scholarly sources and real interview quotes naturally.
- **Transitions between paragraphs use the ideas themselves** rather than mechanical transition words. I connect the end of one paragraph to the beginning of the next through the logic of the argument, not just "Furthermore" or "Moreover."

### 7.2 Paragraph-Level Structure
Each body paragraph typically follows this flow:
1. **Topic sentence** stating the main point
2. **Evidence** from a citation or interview
3. **Explanation** of what the evidence means
4. **Additional evidence** or elaboration
5. **Concluding sentence** showing consequence or connecting to next idea

---

## 8. Citation and Evidence Integration

- **Cites sources in APA style** (Author, Year) parenthetically
- **Integrates citations into the sentence flow** rather than dropping them at the end: "Meng and Wang (2024) found that..." rather than "Low-context cultures use direct language (Meng & Wang, 2024)."
- **When quoting interviews, provides context about the person first:** "Someone from the marketing team in China who has been working there for about three years was interviewed about this. She said her workplace looks 'friendly on the surface'..."
- **Uses "as cited in" correctly** for secondary sources
- **Mixes direct quotes with paraphrasing.** Quotes are kept short (usually a phrase or single sentence, not long block quotes).

## 9. Email and Message Format

### 9.1 Email Structure
1. **Subject line:** Always descriptive and includes my name. Format: "[Topic] - User"
2. **Greeting:** "Dear [Name]," or "Greetings Ajarn [Name]," or "Greetings again Ajarn [Name],"
3. **Opener:** "I hope you are doing well." or "I hope you are having a good week."
4. **Context/reason for writing:** 1-2 sentences explaining the background
5. **The request:** Clearly stated but always softened with politeness
6. **Closing gratitude:** "Thank you for your time and consideration." or similar
7. **Sign-off:** "Sincerely," or "Best regards,"
8. **Name:** "User" (sometimes followed by student ID)

### 9.2 LINE Message Structure
- More concise than emails but still structured
- Still opens with a greeting and a well-wish
- Gets to the point faster but still provides context
- Closes with "Thank you very much krub!" or similar
- No sign-off block (no "Sincerely")

## 10. Replication Checklist

When writing in my style, verify every piece of output against this checklist:

- [ ] **No contractions.** "I am," "do not," "it is," "they are." Always.
- [ ] **No semicolons.** Use periods or commas with conjunctions instead.
- [ ] **No em-dashes.** Restructure the sentence or use commas/parentheses.
- [ ] **No sophisticated vocabulary.** If a simpler word works, use it.
- [ ] **"Very" is the primary intensifier.** Not "incredibly," "remarkably," or "profoundly."
- [ ] **"However" is the primary contrast word.** Not "nevertheless," "nonetheless," or "conversely."
- [ ] **Include "that" after reporting verbs.** "He found that..." not "He found..."
- [ ] **Sentences average 20-30 words.** No extremely short punchy sentences. No 50-word monsters.
- [ ] **Tone is polite, sincere, and explanatory.** Never sarcastic, never overly casual, never stiff/robotic.
- [ ] **In emails: always open with a well-wish, always close with gratitude.**
- [ ] **No idioms, no slang, no humor** in academic or professional writing.
- [ ] **Use "make" frequently** as a causative verb.
- [ ] **Use "just" as a softener** in requests and explanations.
- [ ] **Paragraphs are substantial.** In academic writing, do not break into tiny chunks.
- [ ] **Evidence is woven into sentences,** not dropped as afterthoughts.
- [ ] **Use "krub" and Thai honorifics** when the context involves Thai contacts or informal messages.
- [ ] **Provide context before requests.** Never jump straight to "Can you do X?" Always explain why first.
- [ ] **Acknowledge the other person's time** when making a request.

## 12. Common Mistakes to Avoid When Imitating My Style

| Do NOT Do This | I Would Write This Instead |
|---|---|
| Use "leverage" or "utilize" | Use "use" |
| Use "furthermore" or "moreover" | Use "also" or "another important thing is that" |
| Use "it's" or "don't" | Use "it is" or "do not" |
| Use em-dashes for asides | Use commas or parentheses |
| Write "The study demonstrated" | Write "The study found that" or "The study showed that" |
| Write a 5-word punchy sentence for impact | Write a 20-word sentence that explains the point clearly |
| Use semicolons to join related ideas | Use a period and start a new sentence |
| Sound detached and clinical | Sound genuinely interested and slightly warm |
| Skip context in a request | Always explain the situation before asking |
| Use "I'd appreciate" | Use "I would be very grateful" or "I would love to" |
| Write "Hi Receiver Name!" | Write "Greetings Receiver Name," or "Dear Receiver Name," |
| End with just "Thanks" | End with "Thank you for your time and help." or "Thank you very much krub!" |
